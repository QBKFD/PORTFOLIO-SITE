Project · competition platform

Bots Under Uncertainty

The platform behind a quant hackathon, where teams compete with code instead of clicks. Two games, one question: how well does your bot decide when it can't see everything?

Python · Docker · reconchess · Stockfish · pytest · hypothesis  ·  Repository private, for obvious reasons

The repository holds the house bots, the hidden game parameters and the scoring, so it stays private. This page covers how the platform works and how it was tested, not what's inside the house bots.

  1. 01 · The idea

    Compete with code, not clicks

    Teams get the rules and a starter kit, write a bot, and upload it. The server plays it against house bots and against other teams, and nobody plays by hand. Both games hide something on purpose. In one, you don't know your edge. In the other, you can't see the board. What gets ranked is how well a bot acts on what it can't see.

  2. 02 · Kelly Survival

    Size the bet without knowing the edge

    Each round, a bot bets a fraction of its bankroll on a biased coin. The win probability is hidden and moves, slowly and in sudden jumps, and a signal hints at each outcome with a reliability that also changes. Every bot starts with $1,000 and plays 500 rounds; drop below $10 and it's out. The score is how far it finishes behind an oracle that sees the hidden values, taken as the median over many paths so that one lucky run can't win it.

    Underneath it is the Kelly criterion. Bet too little and you leave growth on the table. Bet too much and the swings eat you, even with a real edge. Slide the bet size and watch what happens to the same 40 coin sequences.

  3. 03 · Reconnaissance Blind Chess

    Play chess on a board you can't see

    Reconnaissance Blind Chess is chess where you never see your opponent's pieces. Each turn you sense one 3×3 window, then move. There's no check: you win by capturing the king. A good bot keeps track of every board the opponent could have and senses where those boards disagree the most. Compute is bounded on purpose: one core, a game clock, a capped engine, and the bot is paused between its turns so it can't think on the opponent's time.

    Below, White has played e4 and Black has replied, but you can't see how. That leaves 21 possible boards. Pick a square to sense and see how many survive.

    Black had 21 options: 16 pawn moves, 4 knight moves, or passing, which the rules allow. The "best sense" is the window that leaves the fewest boards on average.

  4. 04 · The match engine

    A bot can crash. The match can't.

    Every bot runs as its own process, outside the engine, and talks to it only in JSON lines. Each answer passes through a single validation boundary per game. A malformed, late or crashed answer costs a strike: the bot bets nothing or passes that turn. Three strikes and it's out, and the match carries on and writes its replay. The engines themselves are pure: state and action in, new state out, no input or output of their own. So no bot, however broken, can take a match down.

    Four of the 33 chaos bots used in testing. Others flood the output, fork-bomb the container, hog memory, try the network or keep thinking between their turns.

    All randomness is drawn from a seed before any bot acts, and scoring seeds come from a cryptographic source, so nobody can guess them from what they've seen. The same seed and the same bots always give a byte-identical replay, so any result can be replayed and checked.

  5. 05 · Calibration

    Luck must not decide the ranking

    Before a game is allowed on the server, a ladder of reference bots plays it over many seeds, and the ranking is resampled again and again. If the order changes between resamples, luck is deciding and the game's parameters change. Degenerate strategies must lose: a bot that never bets, or always bets the same fraction, has to finish below every bot that tries to learn. Each bot's tier is set from what it does before it ever plays, never from its results.

    Illustrative numbers. With a handful of paths the tiers blur and luck picks the winner. On the real Kelly ladder the tier order held in at least 95% of resamples from 100 paths up, and the leaderboard scores every bot on many more.

  6. 06 · Red teaming

    Attack it before the participants do

    The house bots have to be hard to beat, and the only honest way to check is to attack them. A red team is a person with an AI coding tool, working in a separate session, with exactly what participants get: the rules and the starter kit. Their bots are saved against the clock, at 30 minutes and at the end, and play the house bots over many games. The sessions that build the engine never write these bots, because they know the hidden parts.

    When a red team gets too close, the house bot gets better. A new version replaces the old one only if it passes a promotion rule written down before the games are played. Then a fresh red team goes again.

  7. 07 · Tests

    Tested like it will be attacked

    Tests come first for engine and protocol code, and every milestone ends green. Invariants have their own tests: the bankroll never goes negative, a stake never exceeds the bankroll, and money is counted in whole cents with no floats. Property-based tests throw random input at the engines and the validation boundaries. A layer test fails the build if the engine imports anything it shouldn't. A refactor that shouldn't change behaviour has to reproduce a fingerprint of results.

    Built, tested, red-teamed. Server next.

    Done: both game engines, the participant kits, the replay viewer, the reference ladders, and house bots that have been through red teams. Next: the sandbox checks on a Linux server, then the API and the leaderboard. No participant's code runs on a server until those checks pass.
